The speed of light

Rømer, 1676: Io is late

Jupiter's moon Io slips into Jupiter's shadow every 42.5 hours, regular as a clock. Ole Rømer noticed the eclipses ran late when Earth was far from Jupiter and early when it was near. The extra time is light crossing the extra distance.

Earth to Jupiter – Latest eclipse seen –

Rømer predicted the eclipse of 9 November 1676 would come 10 minutes late, and it did. His numbers implied about 22 minutes for light to cross Earth's orbit; the true value is 16.6.

What you're seeing

Each trip is drawn to scale between Earth on the left and the destination on the right, and the pulse moves at exactly the speed of light, sped up by the playback factor. The ruler at the top uses a log scale so a 1-second trip and a 4-year trip fit side by side.

Mars is the odd one out: its distance changes by a factor of seven as Earth and Mars swing around the Sun, so radio messages take between 3 and 22 minutes.

The scales are hard to hold in your head. The gap to the Moon would fit 30 Earths in a row. The Sun is 390 times farther than the Moon, and Proxima Centauri about 270,000 times farther than the Sun. On the Proxima track, the whole Solar System out to Voyager 1 is squeezed into the first pixel.

Try this

  • Turn on the echo for the Moon. 2.56 seconds is what lunar laser ranging stations time, to a few millimetres.
  • Send light to the Sun in real time and make a cup of tea. The sunlight on your face left the Sun about 8 minutes 20 seconds ago.
  • Try to stop the rover with Mars at its farthest. You can't: it reaches the boulder before your picture of it starting even arrives.

Lunar ranging stations time the echo to about 10 picoseconds, the time light takes to cross 3 mm; since the light goes there and back, that is a 1.5 mm step in distance. The precision comes from averaging many returns, since only a tiny fraction of the photons sent ever make it back from the reflectors left on the Moon.

Why it's strange

Every observer, however they move, measures the same speed for light. That one fact forces time and space to bend to keep it constant, which is Einstein's special relativity.

It also means there is no universal "now". You see the Moon as it was a second ago, the Sun 8 minutes ago and Proxima Centauri over 4 years ago. Looking out is always looking back.

Since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second, so the speed of light is now exact by definition.

The c of relativity is the speed in a vacuum. In water light travels at about c/1.33 and in glass at about c/1.5; nothing that carries energy or information goes faster than c.

The math

t = d / c
c = 299 792 458 m/s

Light time is distance divided by the speed of light. For the Moon, 384,400 km / 299,792 km/s ≈ 1.28 s; for the Sun, 1 AU / c ≈ 499 s, which is 8 min 19 s. The page moves the pulse a fraction (elapsed time ÷ light time) of the way along the track, with elapsed time running at real time multiplied by the playback speed. The ruler at the top places each trip at log₁₀ t.

1 ly = c × 1 yr ≈ 9.46 × 10¹² km

A light-year is a distance: how far light goes in one Julian year of 365.25 days. Proxima Centauri at 4.24 light-years is about 4.0 × 10¹³ km away, so its light time is simply 4.24 years.

c Δt = |rJ − rE| − (RJ − RE)
Δtmax = 2RE / c ≈ 16.6 min

How late an eclipse of Io is seen, compared with when Earth and Jupiter are closest. rJ and rE are the planets' positions, and RJ = 5.2 AU and RE = 1 AU the radii of their orbits. The simulation puts both planets on circular orbits in one plane, fires an eclipse every 1.769 days of Io's orbit, applies this delay, and skips eclipses when Jupiter is within 20° of the Sun, where it could not be observed.

Δx = v × 2d / c

How far a rover moving at speed v drives between the moment your picture shows it and the moment your stop command arrives: the picture is d/c old and the command needs another d/c. At the page's 4.2 cm/s with 12.5 minutes each way, that is 63 m, more than the 50 m to the boulder; at the closest approach (3 minutes) it is about 15 m.

Real-world applications

Where the speed limit shows up

Light is fast enough to feel instant and slow enough to measure. Much of modern technology lives in that gap.

Geophysics

Lunar laser ranging

Apollo 11, 14 and 15 and two Soviet rovers left reflectors on the Moon. Observatories fire laser pulses at them and time the echo, measuring the distance to millimetres. It shows the Moon drifting away by 3.8 cm a year and tests general relativity.

In the demo: Moon with the echo on, 2.56 seconds there and back.
Navigation

GPS

Your phone times signals from several satellites about 20,000 km up. Each nanosecond of timing error is 30 cm of position error, so the satellites carry atomic clocks and even correct for relativity.

In the demo: distance = speed of light × travel time, with times in billionths of a second.
Finance

The race between exchanges

Trading firms built straight microwave links between Chicago and New York because light in glass fibre travels about a third slower than through air. Firms spent hundreds of millions of dollars on new routes to shave a few milliseconds off the 1,200 km trip.

In the demo: at 1 ms per 300 km, only the route and medium are left to improve.
Internet

Why far-away servers feel slow

London to Sydney is about 17,000 km. Even at the speed of light in fibre, a round trip takes well over 100 milliseconds. That is why video calls lag across oceans and why companies copy websites to servers near you.

In the demo: turn on the echo: every question waits for a round trip.
Spaceflight

The Deep Space Network

NASA's big dishes in California, Spain and Australia keep every deep-space probe in view as Earth turns. Commands to Voyager 1 take nearly a day to arrive, and the reply takes nearly a day to return, at a data rate of about 160 bits per second.

In the demo: choose Voyager 1 and wait for the echo.
Robotics

Autonomous rovers

Nobody can joystick a rover across a 20-minute delay. Engineers send a day's plan at once, and Curiosity and Perseverance use their own hazard cameras and AutoNav software to pick safe paths between waypoints.

In the demo: switch on the hazard cameras and the rover stops itself.
