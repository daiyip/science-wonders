Every sound is sines

A square wave has sharp corners and sudden jumps. A sine wave has neither. Yet stack enough sine waves, each one a circle turning at its own speed, and the corners appear. Almost: next to every jump the sum overshoots by about 9%, and no number of extra terms makes that go away.

What you're seeing

Each circle is one sine wave. Its radius is the wave's size, and it turns n times for every single turn of the first circle. Chain the circles end to end and the height of the last tip is the sum of all their heights. That height is drawn across to the right as the bright curve; the faint curve is the wave you are trying to build.

Underneath, the first few harmonics are drawn on their own, and the bars give the size of every harmonic. Those bars are the wave's recipe. In the second mode the same idea runs in two dimensions: circles turning at whole-number speeds trace any closed outline.

The square, sawtooth and triangle use their exact textbook coefficients. A wave you draw is stored as 512 heights over one period, and each coefficient is computed by multiplying those heights by a sine or cosine and averaging, which is the integral in the math section done as a sum. A drawn shape is first resampled to 256 points spaced evenly along its outline, then given a discrete Fourier transform.

Try this

  • On the square wave, set Terms to 1, then 3, 10 and 50. The corners sharpen and the ripples squeeze toward the jumps, but the highest spike stays about 9% of the jump above the top.
  • Switch to the triangle wave. It has corners but no jumps, its harmonics shrink much faster, and three terms already look almost perfect.
  • Press Play sound, then switch between Pure sine and Square wave. The pitch stays the same; only the harmonics change. That difference is what your ear calls the colour, or timbre, of a sound.
  • In Redraw a shape, pick the star. Its sharp points need far more circles than the smooth heart.

With 5 terms the square wave's highest point is about 1.18, an overshoot of 9.1% of the jump of 2. With 50 terms the peak is still 1.18, at 8.95%, but it now sits ten times closer to the jump. The mean error keeps falling, roughly as one over the square root of the number of terms, while the largest error does not fall at all.

Why it's strange

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that any wave, even one with corners and jumps, is a sum of smooth sine waves. Lagrange objected: how can curves with no corners add up to a corner? Both had a point. The sum does approach the jump, but with any finite number of terms it rings and overshoots right next to it, and the overshoot never shrinks below about 9%.

Henry Wilbraham noticed the overshoot in 1848. It became famous in 1898, when Albert Michelson's 80-term harmonic analyser drew it and Josiah Willard Gibbs explained it in letters to Nature in 1898 and 1899.

Mathematicians say the series converges in the mean but not uniformly. The area between the sum and the target goes to zero, because the overshoot gets narrower in proportion to 1/N, but its height stays fixed. Your ear runs a version of the same analysis: the cochlea responds to different frequencies at different places along its length, so you hear a square wave as a fundamental plus a set of fainter, higher tones.

The math

bn = (2/T) ∫0T f(t) sin nωt dt

The Fourier series of a wave with period T. Each pair an, bn becomes one circle of radius √(an² + bn²) turning n times per period, and the Terms slider keeps the first N circles whose radius is not zero. For a drawn wave the page replaces each integral by an average over the 512 stored heights.

f1(t) = (4/π) Σn = 1, 3, 5, … sin(nωt) / n
f2(t) = (2/π) Σn = 1, 2, 3, … (−1)n+1 sin(nωt) / n
f3(t) = (8/π²) Σn = 1, 3, 5, … (−1)(n−1)/2 sin(nωt) / n²

The square wave f1, the sawtooth f2 and the triangle f3, each running between −1 and +1. These are the coefficients the page uses. The two waves with jumps have harmonics that shrink only as 1/n; the triangle, which is continuous, has harmonics that shrink as 1/n², which is why it converges so much faster and shows no lasting overshoot.

The Gibbs overshoot. As the number of terms N grows, the highest point of the square wave's partial sum SN approaches (2/π) Si(π), so the overshoot approaches 8.95% of the jump. The Overshoot readout finds the highest and lowest points of the partial sum on a grid of 2,048 points per period and compares them with the target's range.

ck = (1/M) Σm=0M−1 zm e−2πikm/M

ε² = Σk ∉ K |ck|² ÷ Σk ≠ 0 |ck|²

For shapes, each point is a complex number z = x + iy and the M = 256 resampled points are transformed with the discrete Fourier transform. Each ck is a circle of radius |ck| turning k times per loop, clockwise when k is negative. The page keeps the set K of the largest circles, as many as the Circles slider says, and by Parseval's theorem the squared redraw error ε² is simply the share of |ck|² left out. With all 255 circles the redraw passes exactly through every point.

Real-world applications

Thinking in frequencies

Splitting a signal into sine waves turns hard problems into easy ones: you can see which frequencies matter, keep those and throw the rest away.

Audio

MP3 and streaming music

Audio formats such as MP3 and AAC cut sound into short slices, turn each slice into frequencies with a cousin of the Fourier transform, and spend bits only on the components a listener can actually hear. A 128 kbit/s MP3 is about one eleventh the size of CD audio.

In the demo: drop the small bars and the wave barely changes.
Images

JPEG photos

JPEG splits a photo into 8 × 8 pixel blocks and writes each block as a sum of 64 two-dimensional cosine patterns. Fine patterns that the eye barely notices are stored roughly or not at all, which is where most of the saving comes from and why heavy compression blurs sharp edges into ripples.

In the demo: cutting high harmonics rounds off corners and adds ringing.
Medicine

MRI scanners

An MRI scanner does not take a picture directly. Its coils record how strongly the body's signal contains each spatial frequency, filling a grid called k-space, and a computer turns that grid into an image with an inverse Fourier transform. Ringing lines next to sharp edges in MRI scans are the Gibbs phenomenon.

In the demo: the 9% overshoot at a jump, in a medical image.
Communications

Wi-Fi and 4G

Modern radio links use OFDM: data is spread over dozens to thousands of closely spaced sine-wave carriers sent at once. The transmitter builds the combined signal with an inverse fast Fourier transform and the receiver separates the carriers again with a forward one, many thousands of times a second.

In the demo: the spectrum bars, each one carrying its own data.
Oceans

Tide-predicting machines

Tides are a sum of regular cycles set by the Moon and Sun. In 1872 Lord Kelvin built a machine of pulleys and gears that added ten such cycles to draw a year of tides for a port. Later machines summed about 40 and were used to plan the D-Day landings in 1944.

In the demo: rotating circles adding up, built in brass.
Music

Tuners, synthesisers and song recognition

A guitar tuner finds the fundamental frequency in the sound. Additive synthesisers build instrument tones by stacking harmonics, just like the circles here. Song-recognition apps such as Shazam match a fingerprint made from the strongest peaks of a short recording's frequency content over time.
