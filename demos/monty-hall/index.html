The Monty Hall problem

A car hides behind one door and goats behind the rest. You pick a door, the host opens a goat door, and offers you a swap. Most people stay put. They shouldn't: switching wins two times out of three.

Where the probability goes

The Bayes calculation

Say you pick door 1 and the host opens door 3. Before he acts, each door has a 1/3 chance. Then ask how likely his move was in each case:

Car behind 1: P(opens 3) = ½ (2 or 3, his choice)
Car behind 2: P(opens 3) = 1 (his only goat)
Car behind 3: P(opens 3) = 0 (never the car)
 
P(opens 3) = ⅓·½ + ⅓·1 + ⅓·0 = ½
P(car 1 | opens 3) = (⅓·½) ÷ ½ = 1/3
P(car 2 | opens 3) = (⅓·1) ÷ ½ = 2/3

Bayes' rule weighs each possibility by how well it explains what you saw. If the car is behind door 2, the host was forced to open door 3, so seeing door 3 open is twice as likely in that world. That doubles door 2's odds relative to yours.

What you're seeing

The host knows where the car is. He always opens every other door except one, never shows the car, and always offers the swap. Your first pick is right only 1 time in 3. Switching wins exactly when that first pick was wrong, which is the other 2 times in 3.

The chart replays the game a thousand times. Early on the two lines wander; as games pile up they settle onto the dashed lines at 1/3 and 2/3.

The simulation plays each game with the same rules as the doors above: the car and your pick are each chosen uniformly at random, and a knowing host who has a choice (because you picked the car) leaves a random other door closed. Staying and switching are scored on the same games, so the two lines always add up to 100% when the host knows.

Try this

  • Play ten games always switching, then ten always staying, and compare your tally.
  • Slide to 100 doors. You pick one, and the host opens 98 goats, carefully skipping one door. Would you still stay?
  • Make the host open doors at random. When he happens to show only goats, staying and switching really are 50/50. Games where he reveals the car don't count.

Why it's strange

Two closed doors feel like a coin toss. But the host's choice was not random: he had to avoid the car, and that carries information about the door he skipped and none about yours.

When Marilyn vos Savant gave the switching answer in Parade magazine in 1990, about 10,000 readers wrote in, nearly 1,000 of them with PhDs, most saying she was wrong. The mathematician Paul Erdős reportedly stayed unconvinced until he saw a computer simulation.

The answer depends on the rules, not just on what you see. If the host only offered a swap when you had already picked the car, switching would always lose. The 2/3 answer needs a host who always opens goat doors and always offers the swap, whatever you picked.

The math

P(Cp) = 1/n   ⇒   P(Cs) = 1 − 1/n = (n − 1)/n

Cp means the car is behind your pick, Cs that it is behind the one door the knowing host skips, and n is the number of doors. The host never opens the car, so if your pick is wrong the car must be behind the skipped door. With 3 doors switching wins 2/3 of the time, with 100 doors 99/100. The simulation scores a switch as a win exactly when the skipped door is the car.

P(C2 | H3) = P(H3 | C2) · P(C2) ÷ P(H3) = (1 · ⅓) ÷ ½ = ⅔

Bayes' rule for the classic case: you pick door 1, H3 means the host opens door 3. If the car is behind door 2 he must open door 3, so P(H3 | C2) = 1. Averaged over all three car positions, he opens door 3 half the time, so P(H3) = ½.

P(G) = 2/n   ⇒   P(Cp | G) = P(Cs | G) = (1/n) ÷ (2/n) = ½

For the random host, G means he happened to show only goats. That needs the car behind your pick (chance 1/n) or behind the door he left (also 1/n), so only 2 games in n count, and in those the two closed doors are equally likely. With 3 doors a third of the random-host games are void, and the simulation leaves them out of the "counted" games.

σ = √(p(1 − p) ÷ N) ≈ √(⅔ · ⅓ ÷ 1000) ≈ 0.015

The typical scatter of a measured win rate p after N games. After 1,000 games the simulated lines usually sit within about 1.5 percentage points of 1/3 and 2/3, and within 3 points about 95% of the time. After only 10 games σ is about 15 points, which is why your own tally can easily look 50/50.

Real-world applications

Where updating on evidence matters

The Monty Hall trap is mixing up "how likely is this evidence" with "how likely is my guess, given the evidence". Bayes' rule keeps them apart, and getting it right matters far beyond game shows.

Medicine

Reading a positive test

Suppose 1 person in 1,000 has a disease, and a test catches 99% of cases but also flags 5% of healthy people. A positive result then means only about a 2% chance of disease, because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones. Ignoring that base rate is one of the most common errors in reading screening results.

Email

Spam filters

Bayesian spam filters, popularised by Paul Graham's 2002 essay "A Plan for Spam", start from how common spam is and update on each word in a message. A word like "unsubscribe" shifts the odds a little; dozens of such clues together make the verdict nearly certain.

In the demo: the host's door choice is a clue, weighed by Bayes' rule.
Technology

A/B testing

Websites show two versions of a page to different visitors and compare results. Early numbers swing wildly, and stopping as soon as one looks ahead produces false winners. Bayesian A/B tests report the probability that one version is truly better, given the data so far.

In the demo: watch how far the lines stray before 1,000 games settle them.
Rescue

Finding Air France 447

After the 2009 crash in the Atlantic, two years of searching found no wreck. In 2011 analysts built a Bayesian map of where the plane could be, updating it with every area already searched. The wreck was found within about a week once searching resumed in the area the map ranked highest. The US Coast Guard plans searches the same way.

In the demo: every opened door with no car moves probability onto the doors left.
Law

The prosecutor's fallacy

In 1999 Sally Clark was convicted of murdering her two baby sons, partly on testimony that two cot deaths in one family had a 1 in 73 million chance. That figure was flawed, and it answered the wrong question: double murder is also extremely rare, so the two had to be compared. Her conviction was overturned in 2003.

In the demo: a rare event alone proves little until you weigh the alternatives.
AI · Robotics

Machines that update beliefs

Robot vacuums and self-driving cars track where they are with Bayes filters, updating a map of likely positions after every sensor reading. Many machine learning methods also treat learning as Bayesian updating: start with a prior, then let the data reshape it.
