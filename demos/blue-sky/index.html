Why the sky is blue

Sunlight carries every colour. The molecules of the air knock blue light aside about six times more often than red, so blue reaches you from every direction. Lower the sun and the same scattering strips the blue out of its long path through the air, leaving an orange sun and a glowing horizon.

What you're seeing

The top strip is the whole sky around you, with the sun in the middle and the horizon along the bottom. Every point is coloured by a calculation: sunlight with its measured mix of colours enters the air, molecules scatter some of it toward you, and part of that is lost again on the way.

Below left, single photons from the sun cross the air, which is drawn far thicker than it really is. Each photon's colour is drawn from the sun's spectrum, and its chance of being knocked aside on the way down is the real one for its colour and the current sun height.

The chart compares colours. Grey dotted: sunlight above the air. Orange: what reaches the ground straight from the sun. Blue: light from the sky overhead, scaled to the same height so you can compare its shape.

Air molecules are about a thousand times smaller than the wavelength of light. Particles that small scatter in proportion to 1/λ⁴, so 450 nm blue is scattered about six times more than 700 nm red. Looking straight up, the whole column of air scatters about 20% of the sun's 450 nm light but only about 4% of its 700 nm light.

Try this

  • Press Watch a sunset. Near the horizon the sunlight crosses up to 38 times as much air as at noon. Blue runs out first, so the sun turns yellow, then orange, then red.
  • Switch to Large droplets. Droplets thousands of times bigger than molecules scatter every colour about equally, so the sky turns white, like haze or the inside of a cloud.
  • Slide Amount of air to 0. The sky goes black with the sun still up, as on the Moon. At 3× the noon sky turns pale and the horizon yellowish.
  • Pick Mars. Fine dust that absorbs blue gives a butterscotch sky, yet next to the setting sun the sky turns blue.

With the sun 30° up the air mass is 2.0 and 64% of the blue at 450 nm comes straight through. At 5° the air mass is 10.3 and only 10% does. At 1° it is 26 and 0.3%, while 32% of the red at 700 nm still gets through. That is the red sun.

Why it's strange

Air is clear. The blue is not a colour the air has but sunlight it bounces. A sunset is the same process seen from the other end: the blue scattered out of the evening sun's long path is what makes the sky blue for people farther along it.

Then why is the sky not violet? Molecules scatter violet even more than blue, and in this model the light from overhead really is strongest at about 410 nm. But the sun sends less violet than blue, our eyes are weak at violet, and sky light is a broad mix of every colour that our three kinds of cone cells report together as pale blue. Switch on the eye-sensitivity curve to see where most of the brightness you see comes from.

John Tyndall made blue "skies" in tubes of fine particles in 1869. Lord Rayleigh found the 1/λ⁴ law in 1871 and showed in 1899 that air molecules alone are enough. Gustav Mie solved scattering by larger spheres in 1908.

The colours here come from single scattering only: each photon reaching you was scattered once. Real skies also contain light scattered two or more times, which makes the real sky overhead a little brighter and paler and the horizon a little less bright than shown. Ozone is included because it absorbs some orange light; without it, the sky overhead would turn grey at sunset instead of staying blue.

The math

σ(λ) ∝ 1 ÷ λ⁴
τR(λ) = 0.008569 λ⁻⁴
  × (1 + 0.0113 λ⁻² + 0.00013 λ⁻⁴)

Scattering by a particle much smaller than the wavelength grows as 1/λ⁴. τR is the Rayleigh optical depth of the whole column of air above sea level, with λ in micrometres: 0.22 at 450 nm, 0.097 at 550 nm and 0.037 at 700 nm. The bracket corrects for air's refractive index changing slightly with colour. The Scattering, blue vs red readout is the ratio of τR at 450 and 700 nm, 6.1 for air; pure 1/λ⁴ would give (700/450)⁴ = 5.9.

m(h) = 1 ÷ [ sin h
  + 0.50572 (h + 6.07995°)−1.6364 ]
T(λ) = e−τ(λ) m

The Kasten–Young air mass: how many times more air the sunlight crosses than from straight overhead, for a sun h degrees above the horizon. It is 1 at the zenith, 2.0 at 30°, 5.6 at 10° and 38 at the horizon, where the curvature of the Earth keeps it finite. T is the fraction of each colour that comes straight through. The orange curve is the sunlight above the air times T, and the two straight-through readouts are T at 450 and 700 nm. The photons in the side view are scattered with probability 1 − T for their own wavelength.

L(λ) = F(λ) ∫ β(λ, s) P(Θ)
  × e−τsun(λ, s) − τeye(λ, s) ds
PR(Θ) = 3 (1 + cos²Θ) ÷ 16π

The sky's spectral brightness in one direction: add up the sunlight scattered toward you at every point s along your line of sight, dimmed on its way in from the sun and on its way out to you. β falls with height as e−z/8 km for air, the Earth is round, and Θ is the angle between the sunlight and your line of sight. Molecules use the Rayleigh phase function PR; droplets and Mars dust use a forward-peaked Henyey–Greenstein function. The page evaluates this for 96 × 36 directions and 33 wavelengths in a background worker.

X = Σ L(λ) x̄(λ) Δλ
Y = Σ L(λ) ȳ(λ) Δλ
Z = Σ L(λ) z̄(λ) Δλ

A spectrum becomes a colour through the CIE 1931 colour matching functions, a standard model of human colour vision built from colour-matching experiments. X, Y and Z are then converted to screen RGB, balanced so that sunlight above the air looks white, with exposure that adapts to the sky's brightness and a modest saturation boost for the screen. ȳ is the eye's sensitivity to brightness: the eye-sensitivity curve on the chart is L × ȳ. For the noon sky L peaks near 410 nm, but L × ȳ peaks near 540 nm: most of the brightness you see comes from the middle of the spectrum, and the blue hue comes from the balance between x̄, ȳ and z̄, much as it does between the three kinds of cone cells.

Real-world applications

Reading the colour of light

How strongly light scatters depends on its colour and on the size of what it hits. Turn that around and the colour of scattered light tells you about the air, the dust and the glass it passed through.

Air quality

Measuring haze from the ground

Sun photometers, such as the hundreds in NASA's AERONET network, point at the sun and measure how much light gets through at several colours. Subtracting the known scattering by air leaves the amount of haze, and how that loss changes with colour reveals the size of the particles.

In the demo: the two straight-through readouts are what these instruments measure.
Communications

Why internet fibres use infrared

Tiny density ripples frozen into glass scatter light the same 1/λ⁴ way. That loss is why long-distance optical fibres carry infrared light near 1550 nm, where a good fibre loses only about 4 to 5% of its light per kilometre, so signals can cross oceans with amplifiers spaced tens of kilometres apart.

In the demo: red gets through far more air than blue does.
Photography

Polarising filters

Light scattered by molecules at right angles to the sunlight is strongly polarised. A polarising filter can block much of it, which is how photographers darken a blue sky and make clouds stand out. Bees and some birds use the same pattern of polarisation to navigate.

In the demo: the effect is strongest in the sky 90° from the sun, marked "side".
Climate

Volcanic sunsets

After Krakatoa erupted in 1883, vivid red sunsets were seen around the world for months. Pinatubo in 1991 put about 15 million tonnes of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it formed fine droplets that reflected sunlight and cooled the Earth by about 0.5 °C for more than a year.

In the demo: more particles on the light's path mean a dimmer, redder sun.
Astronomy

Correcting starlight for the air

A star low in the sky looks fainter and redder because its light crosses more air. Astronomers measure this extinction each night and correct for air mass, and they build telescopes on high mountains such as Mauna Kea, at 4200 m, which is above about 40% of the atmosphere.

In the demo: the Air mass readout is the factor they correct for.
Planetary science

Blue sunsets on Mars

In April 2015 the Curiosity rover photographed a blue sunset in Gale crater. Fine Martian dust, with grains a few micrometres across, sends blue light mainly forward, close to the direction of the sun, while the rest of the sky looks butterscotch. Fitting such images tells scientists the size and make-up of the dust.

In the demo: pick Mars and lower the sun.
