Prime spirals

Write 1, 2, 3, … in a square spiral and light up the primes. They should look scattered at random, yet they crowd onto diagonal lines. Every one of those lines is a simple quadratic formula, and why some of them are so full of primes is still an open problem.

What you're seeing

The starting number sits in the centre. The next one goes to its right, and the count winds outward anticlockwise, one ring at a time. Each prime is lit in blue; every other square stays dark. When the cells are big enough, the numbers are written in them.

Click or tap any cell to see its number, its prime factors and its nearest prime neighbours. The chart counts the primes up to each number and compares the count with two famous estimates.

The Sacks spiral puts each number n at distance √n from the centre, making one full turn per whole square root. The perfect squares 1, 4, 9, 16, … all land on the ray pointing right, and the prime-rich formulas become smooth curves.

The page finds every prime up to the largest number shown with a sieve of Eratosthenes that also records each number's smallest prime factor, so any number can be factored instantly by dividing out that factor again and again. For the largest setting, 501 × 501 cells starting at 1,000,000, that is about 1.25 million numbers, sieved in a few hundredths of a second.

Try this

  • Click a number on a bright streak and press Trace. Compare its prime share with the 10% or so of all numbers shown.

The highlight readout compares two numbers. The first is how many times more often the formula's values are prime than random numbers of the same size, measured on the values shown. The second is what the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture predicts for that formula in the long run. For Euler's polynomial the prediction is about 6.6, and the measured value creeps toward it as you grow the grid.

Why it's strange

Primes are fixed by the rule "no divisors except 1 and itself". Nothing in that rule mentions spirals or lines. Yet in a spiral, every diagonal is a quadratic formula like 4n² + 2n + 41, and some quadratics turn out primes far more often than others.

Part of the answer is simple. Odd and even numbers alternate like a chessboard, so half the diagonals hold only even numbers and can never light up. A formula is prime-rich when its values also avoid being multiples of 3, 5, 7 and other small primes. Euler's n² + n + 41 is never a multiple of any prime below 41.

The rest is unknown. Nobody has proved that n² + 1, or any other quadratic, gives infinitely many primes. That question was on Landau's list of unsolved problems in 1912 and is still open.

Euler found his polynomial in 1772. Stanisław Ulam doodled the spiral during a dull talk in 1963, and it made the cover of Scientific American the next year. Robert Sacks drew his version in 1994.

In 1923 Hardy and Littlewood guessed exactly how often a quadratic should be prime, their "conjecture F". The idea is to treat each small prime p independently: if the formula hits a multiple of p less often than a random number does, its values are more likely to be prime, and the boost is multiplied over all primes. The guess matches every computer test so far, but it remains unproved even for a single quadratic. The only polynomials with a proven count are the straight lines an + b, by the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions.

The math

f(n) = 4n² + bn + c
f(n+1) − 2f(n) + f(n−1) = 8

Each step along a diagonal moves one ring further out, and each ring is 8 cells longer than the one inside it. So the gaps between neighbouring values on a diagonal ray grow by 8 every step, which is exactly what makes them a quadratic with leading coefficient 4. The Trace button reads three cells along the ray, solves for b and c, checks the formula against further cells, and then extends it inward as far as it holds.

π(N) ~ N ÷ ln N
π(N) ≈ li(N) = ∫2N dt ÷ ln t

The prime number theorem, proved in 1896: near a number N, about one number in ln N is prime. The chart plots the true count π(x) from the sieve against both estimates. N/ln N falls short by roughly 10% at these sizes and only very slowly catches up, while the logarithmic integral li(N), computed here as the integral from 2, is within 1% by N = 40,000 and keeps getting relatively closer.

#{n ≤ X : f(n) prime} ≈ Cf ∑n ≤ X 1 ÷ ln f(n)
Cf = ∏p (1 − ω(p)/p) ÷ (1 − 1/p)

The Hardy–Littlewood conjecture F, in the general form later stated by Bateman and Horn. ω(p) counts the solutions of f(n) ≡ 0 mod p among n = 0, 1, …, p − 1. A random number is a multiple of p one time in p, so primes where ω(p) = 0 raise the odds and primes where ω(p) = 2 lower them. For Euler's polynomial ω(p) = 0 for every p below 41, and Cf ≈ 6.64. The page measures the ratio of prime values to the sum of 1/ln f(n) over the values shown, and computes Cf from the product over all primes below 100,000, using the discriminant b² − 4ac to find ω(p).

n²+n+41: b² − 4ac = −163
(−163 ÷ p) = −1 for p = 2, 3, 5, …, 37

For a prime p above 2 and a polynomial with a not divisible by p, ω(p) is 1 + (D ÷ p), where D = b² − 4ac and (D ÷ p) is the Legendre symbol: +1 if D is a square mod p, −1 if not. For Euler's polynomial D = −163 is a non-square mod every prime from 3 to 37, so no value is ever a multiple of those primes, and the values are odd as well. That is why the first 40 values are all prime. The same −163 makes eπ√163 astonishingly close to a whole number.

Real-world applications

Where primes do real work

The patterns in the spiral are a puzzle, but primes themselves are workhorses. Much of their usefulness comes from two facts on this page: factoring is easy for small numbers and very hard for big ones, and primes are common enough to find quickly.

Security

RSA encryption

An RSA key is built by multiplying two secret primes, each hundreds of digits long. Multiplying takes a computer a moment. Recovering the two primes from the product is believed to be practically impossible, and that one-way street protects much of the secure traffic on the web.

In the demo: factoring a six-digit number is instant; a 600-digit one is out of reach.
Security

Finding primes for keys

Key generators pick random odd numbers of the right size and test them until one is prime. The prime number theorem says how long that takes: about one 1024-bit number in 710 is prime, so roughly one random odd one in 355, a split second of testing.

In the demo: the chart's N/ln N curve is the same estimate.
Mathematics · Computing

The quadratic sieve

Carl Pomerance's 1981 factoring method looks at the values of a quadratic, x² − N, and keeps the ones built only from small primes. It was the fastest general method for years and factored the 129-digit RSA challenge number in 1994.

In the demo: whether a quadratic's values share small prime factors is exactly what makes a diagonal bright or dark.
Computing

Random number generators

The "minimal standard" generator of Park and Miller (1988) multiplies by 16,807 and takes the remainder after dividing by the prime 2³¹ − 1. With a prime modulus and a well-chosen multiplier, the sequence visits every value from 1 to 2³¹ − 2 before it repeats. Similar generators sat inside many programming libraries.

In the demo: the fair test needs random numbers too, and they make no lines.
Engineering

Gears that wear evenly

If two meshing gears have 12 and 36 teeth, the same teeth meet every turn and any flaw wears into the same spot. Designers choose tooth counts with no common factor, such as 12 and 37, so every tooth meets every other one in turn. Mechanics call the extra tooth a hunting tooth.

In the demo: a number's factors decide which patterns it repeats with.
Biology

Periodical cicadas

Some North American cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground and then emerge together by the billion. One leading idea is that prime cycles rarely line up with the shorter cycles of predators or of other broods. It is a hypothesis, not a settled fact.

In the demo: 13 and 17 share no factor with any smaller cycle.
