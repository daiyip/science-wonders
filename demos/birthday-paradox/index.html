The birthday paradox

How many people need to be in a room before two of them probably share a birthday? Most people guess around 180. The answer is 23. Fill the room and watch the matches turn up.

What you're seeing

Each dot is a person, placed on a ring of 365 days at their birthday. Birthdays are random and equally likely; February 29 is left out. When two people share a day, both dots turn amber.

The white curve is the exact chance that at least one pair matches: P = 1 − 365! / ((365 − n)! · 365n). It passes 50.7% at 23 people and 99.9% at 70. The amber line is the share of simulated rooms that had a match at each size.

Each simulated room starts empty and adds people with random birthdays one at a time until two share a day, then records how many people that took. The amber value at n is the share of rooms whose first match came at n people or fewer. With 1,000 rooms, the random scatter around 50% is about ±1.6 percentage points, so small wiggles around the white curve are expected.

Try this

  • Turn on "Draw a line for every pair". With 23 people there are 23 × 22 ÷ 2 = 253 pairs, and each one is a chance of a match.
  • Slide to 70. Finding a room with no match is now rarer than 1 in 1,000.
  • Run the simulation several times. The more rooms, the closer amber hugs white.

Why it's strange

We picture ourselves in the room: what are the odds someone shares my birthday? With 23 people that is only 1 − (364/365)22 ≈ 5.9%. You would need 253 other people to reach even odds.

But the question is about any pair at all, and pairs grow with the square of the room. Each pair matches with chance 1/365, so the chance of no match is roughly e−pairs/365, which drops below one half once there are about 253 pairs.

Real birthdays are not spread perfectly evenly: births vary by season and by day of the week. Any unevenness makes a match more likely, because the chance of no match is largest when every day is equally likely. So 23 is an upper bound for real people, though the difference is small.

The math

P(n) = 1 − (365/365) · (364/365) · (363/365) ⋯ ((365 − n + 1)/365) = 1 − 365! ÷ ((365 − n)! · 365n)

The exact chance. The second person must miss 1 day, the third must miss 2, and so on; multiply those chances to get the probability of no match at all, then subtract from 1. The page builds the white curve from this product one factor at a time, which avoids the enormous factorials. P(23) = 0.507 and P(70) = 0.999.

P(n) ≈ 1 − e−n(n − 1) / (2 · 365)

The pairs estimate, drawn as the dashed line. There are n(n − 1)/2 pairs, each matching with chance 1/365, and treating them as independent gives (1 − 1/365)pairs ≈ e−pairs/365 for no match. The pairs are not quite independent, but at 23 people the estimate gives 50.0% against the exact 50.7%.

n½ ≈ √(2 · ln 2 · N) ≈ 1.18 √N

Setting the estimate equal to one half gives the room size for even odds when there are N equally likely labels. For N = 365 it gives 22.5, so 23 people. Because it grows with √N, a million labels still collide after only about 1,180 draws. This is the rule behind hash collisions and random IDs.

E[K] ≈ √(πN/2) + 2/3 ≈ 24.6

K is the number of people in the room when the first match appears, exactly the number each simulated room records. Its average for N = 365 is about 24.6, a little above the median of 23 because a few rooms run long. Pressing "Add people until a match" draws one value of K.

Real-world applications

Where collisions come early

Replace 365 days with any number of possible labels, and collisions show up after roughly the square root of that number. Engineers plan around this, and attackers exploit it.

Cryptography

The birthday attack on hashes

A hash with n-bit outputs takes about 2n tries to match one given value, but only about 2n/2 to find any two inputs that match. That halving is why 160-bit SHA-1 was retired. In 2017 Google and CWI Amsterdam published the first real SHA-1 collision: two different PDF files with the same hash.

In the demo: 23 is close to √365; collisions start near the square root.
Software

Random IDs (UUIDs)

A version 4 UUID has 122 random bits, so 5.3 × 1036 possible values. The birthday rule says you would need to make about 2.7 × 1018 of them before the chance of any duplicate reaches 50%. That is why systems can create IDs independently without checking with each other.

In the demo: the pairs estimate 1 − e−pairs/N with N = 2122.
Computing

Hash tables

A hash table drops keys into numbered slots. Even a table with plenty of free space will see two keys land in the same slot surprisingly early, so every practical design includes a plan for collisions, such as chaining entries in a list or probing for the next free slot.

In the demo: 365 slots, 23 keys, a coin-flip chance of a collision.
Software

Short Git commit IDs

Git names every commit by a long hash, but people use the first 7 hex characters. That gives 268 million values, and a project with tens of thousands of commits will contain clashing short IDs. Git now lengthens its abbreviations automatically as a repository grows; the Linux kernel uses 12 characters.

In the demo: a bigger room needs a bigger calendar to stay match-free.
Forensics

DNA database matches

A partial DNA profile might match a random person with odds of one in many millions. But comparing every profile in a database to every other one means billions of pairs, so some partial matches between unrelated people are expected. Searches of state databases in the US found such pairs and forced courts to weigh the difference between the two questions.

In the demo: "matches me" (5.9%) versus "any pair matches" (50.7%).
Everyday odds

Lottery repeats

In September 2009 the Bulgarian lottery drew the same six numbers in two draws four days apart, prompting an investigation. With thousands of lotteries holding draws for decades, some draw somewhere repeating an earlier one is far more likely than any particular repeat.

In the demo: each new draw is another person entering a very large room.
