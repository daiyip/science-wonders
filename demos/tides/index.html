Why there are two tides

The Moon pulls hardest on the side of Earth facing it and least on the far side. Take away the average pull that moves Earth as a whole, and what is left stretches the planet along the line to the Moon. The ocean piles up on both sides, so a town turning underneath meets high water twice a day.

What you're seeing

Earth seen from above the North Pole, turning once a day, with the Moon going round once every 27.3 days. The arrows show the Moon's gravity at twelve points on Earth's surface. The three views show the raw pull, the average pull being taken away, and what is left over: the tidal force.

The blue shell is the ocean. Water settles where the tidal force pushes it, which makes two bulges, one under the Moon and one on the opposite side, with low water in between. The marked town is carried through both bulges and both low belts every day, which the charts on the right record.

The arrows are the exact difference between the Moon's pull at each point and its pull at Earth's centre, worked out from Newton's law, not from an approximation. The ocean, the town's tide and both charts use the equilibrium tide: the shape the water would take if it could settle instantly. Distances on the picture are not to scale. The real Moon is 60 Earth radii away, far beyond the edge of the picture.

  • Drag Moon distance from 60 down to 30 Earth radii. Half the distance gives eight times the tidal acceleration.

A closer Moon also orbits faster (Kepler's third law), so it runs ahead faster and Earth takes longer to catch up with it. At about 36 Earth radii the high tides come 13 hours apart. The page keeps Earth's day at its present length. In reality the Moon was closer long ago and the day was shorter, because tides have been slowing Earth's spin ever since.

Why it's strange

Gravity pulls toward the Moon, yet the sea also rises on the side facing away from it. The whole Earth is falling toward the Moon as the two circle their shared centre of mass. What shapes the water is not the pull itself but how it differs from place to place: the near side is pulled a little more than the centre, the far side a little less, so both are left bulging outward.

Real tides are messier than this picture. Continents stop the bulges from sweeping round the planet, so each ocean basin holds a tide wave that circles inside it. Some bays resonate: the Bay of Fundy in Canada sloshes with a natural period close to 12 h 25 min and its tides reach about 16 m, while much of the Mediterranean sees only a few tens of centimetres.

Newton explained the two daily tides in the Principia in 1687. In 1775 Laplace showed that real tides are waves shaped by the depth and outline of the oceans.

The equilibrium tide is only 0.36 m for the Moon and 0.16 m for the Sun, close to the real open-ocean tide of around half a metre. The ocean cannot keep up with the bulges, and friction drags on it, so high water usually comes hours after the Moon passes overhead and spring tides arrive a day or two after new or full moon. The Moon's orbit is also tilted to the equator, which makes the two daily tides at most places unequal in height. This model leaves both effects out.

The math

atidal = GM ÷ (d − r)² − GM ÷ d²
  ≈ 2GMr ÷ d³

The difference between the Moon's pull on the near side, a distance r = 6371 km closer, and on Earth's centre, a distance d away. Because r is much smaller than d, it is close to 2GMr/d³: about 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ m/s², roughly a nine-millionth of g. The Tidal acceleration readout shows this value. The arrows use the exact difference, which is a few percent larger on the near side than on the far side.

a∥ ≈ +2GMr cos ψ ÷ d³
a⊥ ≈ −GMr sin ψ ÷ d³

At a point at angle ψ from the Earth–Moon line, the tidal force splits into a part along the line, which stretches outward on both the near and far sides, and a part across it, which squeezes inward half as strongly. That is the pattern of the Tidal force arrows. Both parts fall as 1/d³, so halving the distance multiplies them by eight.

h(ψ) = (M ÷ ME) (R ÷ d)³ R
  × (3 cos²ψ − 1) ÷ 2

The equilibrium tide: the height of a water surface that sits level with the tidal force plus Earth's own gravity, with R Earth's radius and ME its mass. For the Moon the bulges rise 0.36 m and the low belts drop 0.18 m. For the Sun, 27 million times heavier but 390 times farther away, the factor is 0.16 m, 46% of the Moon's. The page adds the two and evaluates the sum at the town for the readouts and charts.

Thigh = ½ ÷ (1/TE − 1/TM)
TM = 27.32 d × (d ÷ 60.3 R)3/2

The town meets a bulge each time it turns half a turn relative to the Moon. With Earth's turn relative to the stars TE = 23.93 h and the Moon's orbit TM = 27.32 days, that gives 12.42 h, or 12 h 25 min. The Moon's period follows Kepler's third law, so bringing it closer shortens TM and lengthens the time between high tides.

Real-world applications

Living with the tides

The same stretch that lifts the sea also bends rock, tears comets apart and slows Earth's spin. Predicting it and harvesting it are old crafts.

Energy

Tidal power stations

A barrage across the Rance estuary in France has made electricity from the tide since 1966, with a peak output of 240 MW. South Korea's Sihwa Lake station, opened in 2011, is slightly larger at 254 MW. Both work because the water level rises and falls on a timetable that can be predicted years ahead.

In the demo: the town's two highs a day are what a tidal plant runs on.
Navigation

Tide tables

Ports publish tide times a year in advance by adding up dozens of sine waves, each tied to a motion of the Moon or the Sun. The largest has a period of 12.42 hours from the Moon; the Sun's is exactly 12 hours. Lord Kelvin built a mechanical tide-predicting machine to do these sums in 1872.

In the demo: the 12 h 25 min lunar tide and the 12 h solar tide drift in and out of step.
Coastal flooding

King tides

When a spring tide comes while the Moon is near its closest point to Earth, the water climbs higher than usual. In low-lying cities such as Miami Beach these king tides now flood streets on sunny days, and they make storm surges more dangerous.

In the demo: a closer Moon and a lined-up Sun both raise the tide.
Particle physics

A tide inside an accelerator

In the early 1990s physicists at CERN found that the beam energy of the 27 km LEP collider drifted up and down twice a day. The Moon's tide was stretching the solid rock enough to change the ring's circumference by about a millimetre.

In the demo: the tidal force stretches the whole Earth, rock as well as water.
Astronomy

The Moon is drifting away

Laser pulses bounced off mirrors left on the Moon by Apollo astronauts show it moving away by about 3.8 cm a year. Friction carries the bulges slightly ahead of the Moon; their pull speeds the Moon up and slows Earth's spin, making the day longer by roughly 2 milliseconds per century.

In the demo: move the Moon farther out and watch the tides shrink.
Planetary science

Torn apart by tides

Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 passed so close to Jupiter in 1992 that the difference in Jupiter's pull across it broke it into more than 20 pieces, which hit the planet in July 1994. The same stretch stops ring particles close to a planet from gathering into moons.
