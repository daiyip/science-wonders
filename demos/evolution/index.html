Natural selection you can watch

Nobody designs these creatures. Hawks eat whichever ones they happen to spot, the rest have offspring that are almost but not quite copies of their parents. Within a few dozen generations the population matches its background.

What you're seeing

Each creature has two inherited traits: its shade, from near black to pale cream, and how fast it can run. Hawks wander the ground. Every moment a hawk glances at one creature in range and notices it with a chance that grows with how much it stands out from the ground. A noticed creature runs; if the hawk closes in before it tires, it eats.

At the end of each generation the survivors breed back to 100. Each baby copies one parent's traits with a small random error. Running fast burns energy, so faster creatures raise fewer young. Nothing else is programmed.

Try this

  • Let the colours settle, then change the environment. The population tracks the new background within about 20 to 40 generations.
  • Set mutation to zero and change the environment twice. Selection can only pick from variation that already exists, and once it is used up the population is stuck.
  • Take all the hawks away. Colour stops tracking the background and drifts at random, and speed slowly falls because nothing pays for its cost.
  • Add hawks and watch average speed climb: escape is now worth the energy.

Why it's strange

No creature changes during its life and none is trying to adapt. Random copying errors have no direction at all. Direction appears only because the hawks remove some variants more often than others, and that bias, repeated every generation, builds something that looks designed.

This happened in real moths. Dark peppered moths were first recorded near Manchester in 1848 and made up about 98% of the local population by 1895 as soot blackened the trees. After clean-air laws they became rare again.

Real-world applications

Where selection matters today

Anything that copies itself with variation, under pressure, evolves. That includes the germs and pests we fight, and the molecules and designs we deliberately breed.

Medicine

Antibiotic resistance

An antibiotic is a very efficient predator. The few bacteria that happen to resist it survive and multiply. Alexander Fleming warned of this in his 1945 Nobel lecture. A 2022 study in The Lancet estimated that resistant bacterial infections directly caused 1.27 million deaths in 2019.

In the demo: raise predation pressure and the population shifts faster.
Agriculture

Pests that beat pesticides

More than 500 insect and mite species have evolved resistance to at least one pesticide. Farmers growing insect-resistant Bt crops are required to plant "refuges" of ordinary crops, so susceptible insects survive to breed with resistant ones and slow the spread.

In the demo: survivors are the parents; remove the variation they rely on and adaptation stalls.
Chemistry

Directed evolution

Frances Arnold won the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for breeding enzymes the way farmers breed crops: mutate the gene, test thousands of variants, keep the best, repeat. Evolved enzymes now make drugs and work in cold-wash laundry detergents.

In the demo: mutation plus a selection rule, run over and over.
Engineering

Evolved designs

Genetic algorithms apply the same loop to designs inside a computer. NASA's Space Technology 5 satellites, launched in 2006, carried an antenna whose odd bent-wire shape was evolved by software rather than drawn by an engineer. It was the first evolved hardware flown in space.

In the demo: the histograms are a population searching for a good answer.
Oncology

Tumours that evolve

A tumour is a population of varied cells. Full-dose chemotherapy can kill the sensitive ones and leave resistant ones with no competition. "Adaptive therapy" trials dose more gently on purpose, keeping sensitive cells around to hold the resistant ones in check.

In the demo: heavy predation with low variation leaves a population that is hard to shift back.
Conservation

Hunting as a selective force

During Mozambique's civil war, ivory poachers killed most of the elephants in Gorongosa National Park. Tuskless females were more likely to be spared. A 2021 study found that about 18% of females were tuskless before the war, but about half of the females who survived it were. Heavy fishing has likewise pushed some fish to mature younger and smaller.

In the demo: the hawks are the hunters; whatever they find easiest, they remove.
