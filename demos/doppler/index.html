The Doppler effect

A siren sings one steady note, yet you hear it high as it comes and low as it goes. Each ring of sound spreads out from the spot where it was made, and the siren chases its own rings. Speed it up past the speed of sound and the rings pile into a cone that you hear all at once, as a boom.

Simulation

Drag the listener anywhere. The faint dot marks where the siren was when it made the sound the listener hears right now. With the picture focused, the arrow keys move the listener, + and − change the speed and space pauses.

What you're seeing

The siren drives along the road from left to right. Every ring is one wave crest, spreading at the speed of sound, 343 m/s, from the point where the siren was when it made it. Because the siren keeps moving, it nearly catches up with the rings it sends forward, so they crowd together, while the rings it leaves behind are stretched apart.

Crests that arrive closer together mean a higher frequency, so the listener hears a higher pitch while the siren approaches and a lower one after it passes. The strip below the road plots the pitch the listener hears as a multiple of the siren's own note.

The page keeps a record of where the siren was at every moment. For the listener it finds each moment of emission whose ring is just reaching them now, and compares how fast emission time and arrival time tick. If two crests made 1 ms apart arrive 0.5 ms apart, the pitch is doubled. Only every 8th crest is drawn as a ring; the real siren makes 8 crests between neighbouring rings.

Try this

  • Set the speed to 0. The rings are perfect circles around the siren and the listener hears the true note wherever they stand.
  • Press Play sound and listen to a pass at Mach 0.4. The pitch is high and almost steady on the way in, then slides down quickly as the siren goes by. It does not rise as the siren gets closer.
  • Move the listener far from the road. The slide becomes slower and gentler, because the angle between the siren's path and the listener changes more slowly.
  • Press Mach 1. The rings it sends forward can never get ahead of it, so they pile into a single wall at its nose.
  • Press Mach 2. The listener hears nothing at all until the cone sweeps over them, then a boom, then two sounds at once.

At Mach 0.4 a listener right on the road would hear 1 ÷ (1 − 0.4) = 1.67 times the note on approach and 1 ÷ (1 + 0.4) = 0.71 times it afterwards, a fall of more than an octave. Off the road the heard pitch follows the angle θ between the siren's path and the line to the listener, measured at the moment the sound was made, so the slide happens over the time the angle takes to swing round.

Why it's strange

The siren never changes its note, and the driver hears it steady the whole time. The change exists only for someone the siren is moving towards or away from. And what you hear now is old news: it left the siren when the siren was somewhere else, which is why the pitch is exactly the true note only once the siren is already past you.

Christian Doppler predicted the effect in 1842, for the colours of double stars. In 1845 Buys Ballot tested it with horn players riding a train near Utrecht while musicians on the platform judged the pitch. Chuck Yeager first flew faster than sound in level flight in 1947.

Above Mach 1 the siren outruns its own sound. Every point on the cone is a place where crests made over a stretch of time arrive at the same moment, so their energy lands together as a shock wave: the boom. After it, the listener hears two sounds at once: the one made as the siren receded, at a low pitch, and the one made earlier as it approached, which arrives in reverse order, played backwards.

Light has a Doppler effect too, with a difference. Sound moves through air, so it matters whether the source or the listener is moving relative to the air. Light needs no medium; only the relative speed counts, and time dilation adds a shift even for motion straight across the line of sight. Light from galaxies moving away from us is stretched toward red, the redshift. Most distant galaxies are redshifted, and the further away they are the larger the shift, which is how we know the universe is expanding: see The expanding universe.

The math

ta = te + |L − S(te)| ÷ c

A crest made at time te, when the siren was at S(te), reaches the listener at L at the arrival time ta. The page stores the siren's position at every frame and, for the current time, finds every te whose crest is arriving now by looking for a sign change in c(t − te) − |L − S(te)| along the record. Below Mach 1 there is exactly one; above Mach 1 there are none before the cone arrives and two after it.

f′ = f ÷ |dta ÷ dte|
  = f · c ÷ |c − v cos θ|

Crests made dte apart arrive dta apart, so the heard frequency f′ is the siren's frequency f divided by that ratio. Differentiating the first equation gives the second form, with v the siren's speed and θ the angle between its path and the line from where it was to the listener. The page uses the first form, computed from neighbouring records, so it also works when you change the speed mid-pass. The Heard now readout is this number.

M = v ÷ c
sin α = 1 ÷ M

The Mach number M compares the siren's speed with the speed of sound. Above M = 1 the crests are wrapped by a cone with half-angle α: a crest made a time τ ago has radius cτ, and the siren is vτ further on, so the edge of the cone makes an angle with sine cτ ÷ vτ. At Mach 2, α = 30°. Where cos θ = 1 ÷ M, dta ÷ dte is zero: a stretch of crests arrives at once, which is the boom.

f′ = f ÷ [γ (1 − β cos θ)]
γ = 1 ÷ √(1 − β²)
β = v ÷ c
1 + z = λ′ ÷ λ

The same idea for light, which the page does not simulate. The factor 1 − β cos θ is the same crowding of crests; the extra γ is time dilation of the moving source's clock. Head-on recession gives 1 + z = √((1 + β) ÷ (1 − β)). For galaxies far away, most of z comes from the stretching of space while the light travels, but nearby it reads like a Doppler shift.

Real-world applications

Measuring speed by listening

Any wave that bounces off or leaves a moving thing comes back with its frequency shifted by an amount set by the speed. Measure the shift and you have the speed, without touching anything.

Medicine

Doppler ultrasound

An ultrasound probe sends sound of a few megahertz into the body. Blood cells moving toward the probe return it at a slightly higher frequency, cells moving away at a lower one. The shift, a few kilohertz, gives the speed and direction of blood flow, which is how doctors find narrowed arteries and check an unborn baby's heart.

In the demo: moving toward the listener raises the pitch, moving away lowers it.
Traffic

Radar speed guns

A police radar gun sends microwaves at a car and listens to the echo. A car at 100 km/h shifts a 24 GHz signal by about 4.4 kHz, and the gun turns that into a speed. Pointing at an angle reads only the part of the speed along the beam, so the gun reads a little low.

In the demo: only v cos θ counts, the part of the motion toward the listener.
Meteorology

Weather radar

Doppler weather radars measure how fast rain and hail move toward or away from the dish. Next to each other, strong inbound and outbound winds reveal a rotating storm, and forecasters can issue tornado warnings minutes before a funnel touches down.

In the demo: the heard pitch tells you how fast the source closes in.
Astronomy

Finding planets round other stars

A planet makes its star wobble. The star's light shifts toward blue and then red in a regular rhythm, by an amount set by a speed of only metres per second. In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found 51 Pegasi b this way, the first planet seen orbiting a Sun-like star.

In the demo: a source moving toward you is heard higher, moving away lower.
Aviation

Taming the sonic boom

The United States banned supersonic airliner flights over land in 1973, because the boom rattled windows along a strip tens of kilometres wide under the flight path, so Concorde flew faster than sound only over the sea. NASA's X-59 is shaped so that its shock waves spread out instead of merging, aiming for a soft thump instead of a crack.

In the demo: above Mach 1 every listener under the path hears the cone arrive.
Biology

Bats that correct for their own speed

Horseshoe bats hunt with calls near 83 kHz and have ears tuned very sharply to that frequency. Flying toward a wall raises the echo's pitch, so they lower their call just enough that the echo stays in tune, and the flutter of insect wings stands out as a Doppler wobble.

In the demo: f′ = f · c ÷ (c − v cos θ), used in reverse.
