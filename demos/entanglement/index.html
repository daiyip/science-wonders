The Bell test

Alice randomly picks 0° or 45°, Bob randomly picks 22.5° or 67.5°, for 20,000 pairs. Score each setting by how much results agree (+1 all agree, −1 all disagree), then combine: S = E(0°,22.5°) + E(45°,22.5°) + E(45°,67.5°) − E(0°,67.5°). Any theory where photons carry instructions from the source scores at most 2.

What you're seeing

A polarizer lets a photon through or blocks it. For one photon on its own, it's always 50/50. For an entangled pair, if Alice and Bob set the same angle they always agree. Set them 90° apart and they always disagree. In between, quantum mechanics says they agree with probability cos²(angle difference).

The "hidden instructions" universe is Einstein's hope: each pair leaves the source carrying a shared secret, say a preferred angle, and each photon just follows it. That plan can match the 0° and 90° cases perfectly, but in between it can only manage a straight line.

How the page runs each universe: in the quantum one, Alice's photon passes with probability ½, and Bob's result copies hers with probability cos²(a − b). This reproduces the predictions for the entangled state exactly, but it is a recipe, not a mechanism: it uses both angles at once. In the hidden-instructions one, each pair carries a random angle λ, and each photon passes if its polarizer is within 45° of λ. Each photon looks only at its own polarizer and λ, so this universe is local by construction.

Try this

  • Set both polarizers equal. Watch the two tapes match, flip for flip.
  • Put them 22.5° apart. Quantum gives 85% agreement, instructions give 75%.
  • Switch universes and press Sweep. The cos² curve bulges above the straight line, and that bulge is what no local theory can produce.

At 22.5° the gap is cos²(22.5°) ≈ 0.854 against 1 − 22.5/90 = 0.75. The gap is largest near 20°, and the Bell test below uses settings 22.5° and 67.5° apart, which is the choice that pushes the quantum score highest.

Why it's strange

John Bell proved in 1964 that no theory with local hidden instructions can score above 2. Experiments starting with Clauser (1972) and Aspect (1982), and loophole-free tests in 2015, keep getting about 2.8. Nature is not running on hidden instructions.

It still can't be used to send a message: Bob's own results are pure coin flips whatever Alice does. The correlation only shows up when they compare notes.

The Bell test sorts its 20,000 pairs randomly into the four settings, about 5,000 each. Each agreement score then carries random noise of about ±0.01, and S about ±0.02, which is why repeated runs of the hidden-instructions universe land a little above or below 2. Real experiments had to close loopholes this page does not have: detectors that miss photons, and settings chosen slowly enough for a signal to pass between the stations.

The math

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|HH⟩ + |VV⟩) ÷ √2
P(A = B) = cos²(a − b)

The pair's polarization state: both horizontal or both vertical, in an equal superposition. For polarizers at angles a and b, quantum mechanics gives probability cos²(a − b) that both photons do the same thing (both pass or both blocked). Each photon alone still passes with probability ½ at any angle, so neither side can see what the other chose.

E(a, b) = P(A = B) − P(A ≠ B)
  = cos 2(a − b)

The correlation score used in the Bell table: +1 if results always agree, −1 if they always disagree, 0 if they are unrelated. For the quantum state it is cos 2(a − b), so E = +0.707 at 22.5° and −0.707 at 67.5°.

A = +1 ⇔ |a − λ| < 45° (mod 180°)
P(A = B) = 1 − |a − b| ÷ 90°
E(a, b) = 1 − |a − b| ÷ 45°

The hidden-instructions universe: λ is a random angle shared by the pair, spread evenly over 0° to 180°. The two photons disagree only when λ lies inside one polarizer's 45° window but not the other's, which happens for a fraction |a − b| ÷ 90° of all λ. This gives the straight line on the chart (for differences between 0° and 90°). It matches the quantum curve at 0°, 45° and 90° and falls short everywhere else.

S = E(a, b) + E(a′, b)
  + E(a′, b′) − E(a, b′)
|S| ≤ 2
SQ = 2√2 ≈ 2.83

The CHSH form of Bell's inequality, with a = 0°, a′ = 45°, b = 22.5°, b′ = 67.5°. In any local theory each photon's result is fixed by λ and its own setting, and the four terms can then add up to at most 2. The quantum values give 3 × 0.707 + 0.707 = 2√2, the largest any quantum state allows (Tsirelson's bound). The hidden-instructions model gives 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 − (−0.5) = 2, right at the limit.

Real-world applications

Putting spooky action to work

Entanglement can't send a message by itself, but correlations that no hidden-instruction plan can fake turn out to be a resource. These are the uses being built now.

Security

Eavesdropper-proof keys

In entanglement-based quantum key distribution, Alice and Bob turn their matching results into a shared secret key. Any spy who intercepts the photons has to measure them, which breaks the correlations, and a Bell test shows it. China's Micius satellite shared entangled photons between ground stations 1,200 km apart in 2017.

In the demo: equal angles give perfectly matching tapes, a ready-made shared key.
Cryptography

Certified random numbers

If a Bell test scores above 2, the results cannot have been written in advance, so they are provably unpredictable. NIST and the University of Colorado run a public randomness beacon built on this idea, useful for lotteries, audits and fair selection.

In the demo: each tape on its own is a fair coin no one could have scripted.
Computing

Quantum computers

A quantum computer's power comes from qubits that are entangled with each other, so the machine works on correlations that no list of classical values can describe. Quantum error correction also uses entanglement to spread one qubit's information across many.

In the demo: the cos² curve is the extra correlation classical bits can't reach.
Networks

Quantum teleportation

Using a shared entangled pair plus an ordinary message, a quantum state can be moved from one place to another without sending the particle itself. First shown in 1997, it is the basis for quantum repeaters that would link quantum computers into a network.

In the demo: the effect only appears once Alice and Bob compare notes.
Measurement

Better clocks and sensors

Independent atoms in a clock each add their own random noise. Entangling them lets the noise partly cancel, so the clock ticks more precisely than the limit for independent atoms. The same idea is being used to sharpen magnetic and gravity sensors.

In the demo: correlated results are more predictable as a group than as individuals.
Physics

Testing reality itself

Bell tests turned a philosophical argument into an experiment. The 2015 loophole-free tests in Delft, Vienna and Boulder, and the 2022 Nobel Prize for Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger, settled that nature does not run on local hidden instructions.
